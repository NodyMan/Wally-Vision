=== PASTE THIS INTO ELEVENLABS ===

In 1930, Clyde Tombaugh found Pluto by flipping between two photos of the night sky, and spotting the one dot that moved.

Today, NASA's Sphere-X telescope maps the whole sky every six months, in one hundred and two infrared colours. Somewhere in that archive are fast-moving brown dwarfs, asteroids, and maybe even a Planet X.

But a whole year of motion is about one pixel, and the raw files are hard to open. SkyBlink makes it simple.

Type a star's name, or pick one of five ready objects.

SkyBlink finds every Sphere-X visit of that patch of sky, lines them up pixel to pixel, and plays them like a flipbook.

Difference mode subtracts the first visit from the last. A star that moved leaves a black and white pair.

And SkyBlink measures it. Epsilon Indi B moved six point oh, plus or minus one point one arcseconds. The catalogue predicts six and a half.

Barnard's Star is so bright that its centre saturates the detector, so SkyBlink masks the core and still measures the shift: about twelve arcseconds.

Sphere-X sees in a hundred and two colours. Slide through the bands, and watch the same sky change across the infrared.

One click gives a spectrum, with water and methane marked, and a light curve of brightness over time.

And when an honest number isn't possible, SkyBlink says so. R W Aurigae is too bright for this band, so its light curve is marked as saturated, not guessed.

New to astronomy? Simple mode hides the expert tools, and a short trainer teaches the difference between real motion, a pulsing star, and a detector glitch.

Found something interesting? Press "I found something" to save it to the community feed, as a candidate to check.

SkyBlink works in English, Uzbek and Russian, on your desktop and on your phone.

SkyBlink. See the sky move.
